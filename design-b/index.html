Four corners, one stall

“I give you the four corners of the world, because no one can know where they will die.”

The Tuareg are nomadic people of the Sahara, and their smiths are known for silver engraved by hand, line by line. The Agadez cross, with its four arms, is said to point to the four corners of the world. Fathers pass it to their sons with these words.

We took the saying to heart. For years our stall at Camden Lock gathered stones from the four corners: greenstone from Aotearoa, silver and turquoise from the Sahara, amber from the Baltic shore, opal from the Australian outback. Distances above are measured from the stall.

Arahura River, Aotearoa New Zealand
42.7° S, 171.0° E. 18,800 km from Camden Lock

Pounamu

New Zealand's South Island is called Te Waipounamu, the waters of greenstone. The stone is found as boulders in West Coast rivers like the Arahura, and Ngāi Tahu are its kaitiaki, its guardians.

Carved into koru, hei matau and toki, pounamu is traditionally given rather than bought for yourself, and passed down through families. Our stall held the largest collection of carved pounamu outside New Zealand.

Silver and turquoise

Tuareg smiths cut their patterns into silver by hand: triangles, chevrons and rows of dots, built up stroke by stroke like the plate at the top of this page. Pieces were made to be worn on the move, across the desert.

Here Tuareg silver sits beside turquoise strands and inlaid rings, the stone the colour of a desert sky.

Amber

Baltic amber is resin from forests that grew around 40 million years ago. Storms still wash it up on the shores of the Baltic Sea.

Hold a piece to the light and you might find an insect that landed in the resin and never left. The block in this photo has one.

Opal

Opal forms where water carried silica deep underground and left it to set, layer on layer. Its flashes of colour come from light bending through tiny spheres of silica packed inside.

Most of the world's precious opal comes from the Australian outback. Ours sits alongside moonstone and labradorite, stones that also change as you turn them.

Every road led to Camden Lock

For years, Jewellery of the Earth was a stall in Camden Lock Market, its walls hung with hundreds of pendants and its trays full of rings. Travellers stopped to find stones from places they'd been, or wanted to go.

The stall has closed. The collection has moved online, and every piece is still chosen by hand.
